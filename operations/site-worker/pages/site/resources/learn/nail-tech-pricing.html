How to price nail services without guessing

A practical framework for independent nail technicians who want prices based on their real costs and work—not someone else’s Instagram menu.

← All free lessons

Start with the business behind the nail set

A question nail technicians ask constantly is, “How much should I charge for acrylic nails, gel extensions or nail art?” The most useful answer is not a universal price. It is a repeatable process for estimating the real cost of delivering your own service, the compensation you need and the margin your business requires.

Whether you specialize in acrylic extensions, Gel-X, structured gel manicures or custom art, the calculation should account for more than the minutes spent polishing. Setup, consultation, sanitation, cleanup, product use, processing time and business overhead all affect your actual operating cost.

A simple nail tech pricing formula

Starting price floor = (service hours × target labor rate) + consumable materials + allocated overhead.

Then consider additional profit, taxes, complexity, demand, local regulations and your actual market positioning before setting the price you quote.

  1. Record the entire service time. Include setup, prep, client consultation, nail service, cleanup and any practical time allocation for admin. A 90-minute application may occupy more of your working day than 90 minutes.
  2. Calculate supplies used per service. Estimate the real quantity of acrylic, gel, tips, files, gloves, disinfectant and other consumables used. Large bottle prices are not the per-client cost.
  3. Determine the labor target. Choose a rate consistent with your own income needs and realistic billable hours. It is a target to test against demand, not a guaranteed market rate.
  4. Allocate overhead. Include the share of rent or suite cost, software, licensing, insurance, utilities, equipment replacement and payment fees attributable to services.
  5. Review profit and position. Check whether the calculated floor covers your intended margin and whether the service is meaningfully differentiated.

Worked example: a two-hour service

Suppose a service takes two billable hours, the labor target is $30 per hour, materials cost $14, and allocated overhead is $20. The initial cost floor is $94: $60 labor + $14 supplies + $20 overhead. This is an illustration of calculation inputs, not a recommendation to charge $94 or a claim that every nail set has the same costs.

If you add detailed nail art that takes an additional 45 minutes and uses more materials, record those inputs separately. If you adjust prices later, you can explain what changed rather than guessing again.

How to set prices for nail art, Gel-X and acrylic fills

Separate a base service from complexity

Write out what the base price includes. Then define when additional length, shapes, art complexity, design changes or repairs require extra time or supplies. A clear menu prevents misunderstanding and helps customers select accurately.

Review actual appointments

After a few completed appointments, compare estimated service time with real time. A recurring underestimation is information you can use to refine the menu and improve efficiency.

Explain the result, not only the number

Customers may not understand that structured gel, intricate hand-painted designs and maintenance services require different preparation and expertise. Use your menu and educational content to clarify expectations without shaming price-sensitive customers.

Pricing mistakes to avoid

  • Copying competitors’ prices without knowing their costs, market, experience or service scope.
  • Charging only for visible service time and ignoring cleaning, admin, supplies and overhead.
  • Failing to account for non-billable hours, cancellations and slower booking weeks.
  • Keeping prices fixed even after material costs or service time change materially.
  • Using a single average for very different designs and service complexity.
